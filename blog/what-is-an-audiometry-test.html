What Is an Audiometry Test? Procedures, Expectation & Results

If you have been recommended for a "hearing test," you might wonder what the appointment entails, whether it hurts, and what the graphs actually mean. An audiometry evaluation is an objective, non-invasive battery of clinical tests designed to measure the absolute quietest sounds you can detect across the entire audible frequency spectrum.

The Gold Standard in Hearing Diagnostics

Unlike informal online smartphone hearing checks, a comprehensive clinical audiometry test assesses both the quantity of sound you detect (your hearing thresholds in decibels) and the quality of your sound comprehension (your word recognition score in speech environments).

Why a Soundproof Chamber Is Non-Negotiable

Accurate audiometry requires a strictly calibrated acoustic environment. Testing hearing in a regular doctor's office or quiet bedroom is fundamentally flawed because ambient room noise (air conditioning hum, street traffic, fluorescent ballasts) masks high-frequency and low-frequency tones by 15 to 25 dB.

At SoundBridge Clinic in Nagarbhavi, all audiometric evaluations are performed inside an ANSI-certified acoustic testing chamber constructed with multi-layered sound isolation barriers and anti-vibration floating floors.

The Core Diagnostic Audiometry Battery

A standard clinical consultation encompasses three interrelated diagnostic procedures:

  1. Otoscopic Video Examination: The audiologist visually inspects your ear canal and tympanic membrane (eardrum) using high-definition magnification to rule out impacted cerumen (earwax), foreign bodies, or active middle ear infections.
  2. Pure Tone Air Conduction Testing: Wearing calibrated insert earphones or circumaural headphones, you listen for soft pure tones across frequencies from 250 Hz (deep bass) to 8,000 Hz (high treble). You respond by pressing a response button or raising your finger each time you detect a sound.
  3. Bone Conduction Testing: A small oscillator is gently placed on the mastoid bone behind your ear. This bypasses the outer and middle ear to stimulate the cochlear fluid directly, allowing audiologists to determine whether hearing loss is conductive (eardrum/ossicle issue) or sensorineural (inner ear nerve issue).
  4. Speech Discrimination Testing (SDT/WRS): You repeat standardized single-syllable phonetically balanced words presented at comfortable volume levels to evaluate your brain's speech clarity percentage.

How to Read & Understand Your Audiogram

Your results are plotted on a clinical chart called an audiogram:

  • The Horizontal Axis (Pitch / Frequency in Hertz): Ranges from low pitch on the left (250 Hz) to high pitch on the right (8,000 Hz), similar to the keys on a piano.
  • The Vertical Axis (Loudness / Intensity in Decibels): Ranges from very soft sounds at the top (-10 to 20 dB HL) to intensely loud sounds at the bottom (100 to 120 dB HL).

Clinical Hearing Degrees

Normal Hearing: 0 to 25 dB • Mild Hearing Loss: 26 to 40 dB • Moderate Loss: 41 to 55 dB • Moderately-Severe Loss: 56 to 70 dB • Severe Loss: 71 to 90 dB • Profound Loss: 91+ dB.

From Test Results to Personalized Care

At the conclusion of your evaluation, our senior audiologist reviews your audiogram in plain, understandable language. If your thresholds indicate sensorineural hearing loss, we demonstrate live digital hearing technologies tailored specifically to your acoustic prescription, guiding you toward restored clarity and renewed confidence.
